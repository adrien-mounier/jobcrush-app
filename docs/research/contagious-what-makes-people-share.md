# Contagious (Jonah Berger) — what makes people share, and what survives contact with job search

_Written 2026-08-22. Charted by **[#275](https://github.com/adrien-mounier/jobcrush-app/issues/275)** — the wayfinder map "give people a win worth showing off"._

**Provenance, honestly.** I did not read the print book. This crystallises: Berger's own primary
research paper (Berger & Milkman, *What Makes Online Content Viral?*, Journal of Marketing Research
2012 — read in full), Berger's Knowledge@Wharton interview, and three independent detailed
chapter-note sets that agree with each other on the framework and the case studies. Where the
sources disagree on a detail I say so. Everything in Part 3 is my own translation to this product
and is **not** Berger — treat it as argument, not authority.

## Part 1 — The book's actual claim

### The premise Berger starts from

Word of mouth drives **20–50% of all purchasing decisions**. It beats advertising on two counts: it
is trusted (a friend has no incentive to lie) and it is targeted (people only pass a thing to the
person they think wants it — no targeting engine required).

The counter-intuitive one, and the one most people get wrong: **only ~7% of word of mouth happens
online.** The overwhelming majority is spoken, one-to-one, face to face or in a private message.
Berger's point is that "viral" is the wrong mental image — most transmission is a person telling
one other person, quietly. Designing only for a public feed is designing for 7% of the channel.

His central claim: virality is **not** luck and it is **not** a property of charismatic people
("influencers"). It is a property of the *message*, and it is engineerable. Six ingredients.

### STEPPS

#### S — Social Currency: *"We share things that make us look good"*

People are curating an impression of themselves. Sharing is a currency they spend on that. So the
question is never "is my product interesting to me" — it is **"what does passing this on say about
the person who passed it on?"**

Three sub-mechanisms:

- **Inner remarkability.** Every product has something in it that breaks a pattern — novel,
  surprising, extreme. Find it and lead with it. Blendtec was a boring blender company; "Will It
  Blend?" (blending an iPhone) has 150M+ views. The blender didn't change; the frame did.
- **Game mechanics.** Rules, levels, feedback, comparison. Airline frequent-flier tiers exist so
  people can *say* they are Gold. Achievement is only social currency if there is a scale to be
  high on and a way to mention it without appearing to boast — the status has to be *conferred*,
  so the person is reporting a fact rather than claiming a virtue.
- **Insider status / scarcity.** Please Don't Tell, the NYC speakeasy behind a hot dog shop's phone
  booth, 45 seats, no advertising, permanent queue. Secrecy is shareable *because* passing it on
  proves you were inside.

The caveat Berger flags, which matters: **paying for referrals kills them.** Money reframes a
generous act as a transaction and the intrinsic motive dies.

#### T — Triggers: *"Top of mind, tip of tongue"*

Social currency gets the first mention. Triggers get the second and the hundredth. A trigger is a
stimulus already present in someone's daily environment that fires the thought of your product.

- **Frequency vs. link strength is a real trade-off.** Coffee is a daily trigger; hot chocolate is
  a seasonal one — coffee wins on frequency. But a very common cue (the colour red) is linked to
  hundreds of things and fires weakly for any one of them. The best trigger is *frequent AND
  uniquely yours*. Kit Kat deliberately welded itself to the coffee break — a cue that arrives
  every working day and, once trained, belongs to them.
- Rebecca Black's "Friday" gets re-fired every seven days by the calendar itself, free, forever.
- The 1997 Mars mission spiked **Mars bar** sales. No campaign, no connection — the news cycle was
  a trigger the company didn't build and couldn't have bought.

The lesson with teeth: a campaign that peaks and dies had social currency and no trigger.

#### E — Emotion: *"When we care, we share"* — but arousal, not valence

This is the part with hard data behind it, from Berger & Milkman's paper: **~7,000 New York Times
articles over three months**, testing what predicts making the "most emailed" list, controlling for
where the article was placed on the page, time of day, author, and length.

The finding that overturned the received wisdom: **what predicts sharing is physiological arousal,
not whether the feeling is positive or negative.**

| High arousal → **more** sharing | Low arousal → **less** sharing |
|---|---|
| Awe (the strongest single positive predictor) | Sadness (actively suppresses sharing) |
| Excitement, amusement | Contentment |
| Anger | Relaxation |
| Anxiety | |

Positive content out-shares negative content overall — but a *high-arousal negative* (anger)
out-shares a *low-arousal positive* (contentment). Arousal is the axis; valence is secondary.

These held after controlling for how surprising, how interesting, and how practically useful the
article was — each of which independently raised sharing too.

Second-order finding worth remembering: arousal from an *unrelated* source still increases sharing.
People who had just exercised, or just been through turbulence, shared more of whatever was in
front of them. The physiological state travels.

Practical instruction: **"focus on feelings."** Not features, not statistics — the thing the person
feels. And if the honest emotion of your moment is a low-arousal one, you have a sharing problem
that no share button fixes.

#### P — Public: *"Built to show, built to grow"*

People imitate what they can see, and they can only see what is observable. Two devices:

- **Self-advertising.** The product broadcasts its own use as a side-effect of being used. The
  glowing Apple logo faces outward. The Hotmail signature line. iPod's white earbuds against a
  world of black ones.
- **Behavioural residue.** The physical trace an action leaves behind, after the action is over.
  "I Voted" stickers. Livestrong's yellow band — Berger notes the colour choice was doing work:
  rare, gender-neutral, impossible to miss. Movember turns a face into a billboard for a month.
  Lululemon's bag gets reused for groceries for a year.

The inverse is the real lesson: **a great private experience has no growth engine.** Berger's
example of the failure mode is anti-drug campaigns that made drug use look ubiquitous — visibility
is neutral, it amplifies whatever it makes visible, including the thing you're fighting.

#### P — Practical Value: *"News you can use"*

People share useful things because helping someone feels good and reflects well on the helper. This
is the most reliably durable of the six — it doesn't need a moment or a mood.

- **Narrow beats broad.** Counter-intuitively, narrowly-targeted useful content is shared *more*,
  because a specific thing instantly summons the specific person it's for. "Useful to everyone" is
  useful to no identifiable recipient, so it triggers no send.
- **Reference points and diminishing sensitivity.** £20→£10 delights; £1,000→£990 doesn't. Same £10.
  Value is judged against a reference point, not in absolute terms.
- **The Rule of 100.** Under £100, express a discount as a **percentage** ("20% off"). Over £100,
  express it in **absolute money** ("£250 off"). Whichever number looks bigger.
- Scarcity and restriction raise perceived value — and give the recipient something to act on now.

#### S — Stories: *"Information travels under the guise of idle chatter"*

People don't transmit information; they transmit narrative, and information rides along inside it.
The Trojan Horse.

The failure mode Berger is emphatic about: **valuable virality.** A stunt that everyone watches and
no one connects to you is a wasted spend. He contrasts the man-in-a-gorilla-suit school of viral
advertising — memorable, brand-detachable — with Panda Cheese ("Never say no to Panda"), where the
joke *cannot be retold* without naming the brand. Subway's Jared worked for the same reason: the
weight loss is unretellable without the sandwiches.

The test: **can someone retell this story and leave you out of it?** If yes, you built a story for
someone else.

## Part 2 — The three modern cases the owner named, and what each actually did

These are not in the book (2013 predates all three). They are the framework in the wild.

### Wordle — Social Currency + Triggers, and a share format that solved one specific problem

Josh Wardle built it for one person. Under 100 players in early November 2021; ~300k by early
January; tens of millions by February.

The thing that turned it is precisely datable: **on 2021-12-16 he shipped the emoji-grid share
button** — an idea he took from players in New Zealand who were already hand-building the grids.
Growth went vertical from there.

Why that specific format worked, and it is worth being exact because the lesson is transferable:

1. **It proves the achievement without stating it.** Three rows ending in green means you got it in
   three. The person never types "I'm clever" — the grid says it. This is Berger's conferred-status
   point: status you *report* is bragging, status the artefact *demonstrates* is a fact.
2. **It solves the spoiler problem.** You can post your result without ruining the puzzle for the
   person reading. Without this, every share would have poisoned the well and sharing would have
   been antisocial. **The share format removed the reason not to share** — that is the whole trick,
   and it is the part most people miss when they copy Wordle.
3. **One puzzle a day is the trigger**, and the scarcity is what makes the comparison meaningful:
   everyone did the *same* puzzle, so everyone's grid is commensurable. Unlimited play would have
   destroyed both the trigger and the comparability.
4. **The grid contained no link.** All that growth came from people who saw a grid and went
   *looking*. Optimising the share for attribution would likely have made it worse, not better.
5. Zero activation cost: a web page, no app, no signup, no ads.

### Strava — Public, made from effort

Strava's move is turning a private act into an artefact: the route map, the distance, the pace, the
elevation. What gets posted is not "I am fit" — it is **evidence of effort**, which is the socially
safe thing to display. Nobody resents a person for having gone running. The map is behavioural
residue: the run is over, the trace persists and stays visible.

Note the ordering: the effort was already real. Strava didn't manufacture an achievement, it made
an existing one *legible*. Segments and leaderboards are Berger's game mechanics layered on top.

### Spotify Wrapped — identity, not achievement

The angle Wrapped takes is different and it is the one the owner spotted: it isn't a win, it's a
**self-portrait**. Nobody "achieved" listening to a band. It spreads because posting it says
something true about who you are, and people like being seen accurately.

Its design properties:

- **The card is about the user, not the brand.** Spotify's logo is small. The user is the content.
- **Optimal distinctiveness** — the reason it lands psychologically. People want to belong *and*
  stand out simultaneously, and Wrapped delivers both in one card: "I'm in the top 1% of Wilco
  listeners" is membership and rarity at once.
- **It is annual and dated.** Scarcity plus a calendar trigger, and everyone posts in the same
  week, so posting is normal rather than self-regarding. Social proof for the act of sharing.
- **It closes a loop**: knowing December is coming changes listening behaviour all year.
- Reported effect: **+21% mobile downloads** in the first week of December 2020.

**The common structure across all three:** the user gets a compact artefact, *about them*, that
demonstrates something they'd want known, in a format where posting it doesn't read as boasting —
and the product's name comes along because the format is unmistakably ours.

## Part 3 — Contact with reality: job search is the one domain where this inverts

This part is my argument, not Berger's. It is the reason this cannot be copied straight.

### The inversion

Every case above shares one property: **the underlying activity is one the sharer is proud of.**
Running, listening to music, solving a puzzle. Sharing the artefact reveals the activity, and
revealing the activity is fine.

Job searching is not that activity. It is a **concealable stigma**, and it is concealed:

- **84%** of unemployed people believe a stigma exists; **67%** believe it is actively hurting
  their chances of being hired.
- **27%** have not asked a single person they know for help — from embarrassment, or a belief that
  nobody would help.
- The academic framing (Journal for Labour Market Research, 2019) is exact: unemployment is a
  stigma that is *not highly visible and therefore concealable*, and people manage it by choosing
  not to disclose — except that job search **forces** disclosure to get hired. Job seekers live in
  that squeeze permanently.

And we have a natural experiment already run at scale, on precisely the feature a naive reading of
this book would build. **LinkedIn's #OpenToWork banner**: ~220 million people have used it, and it
is the most contested feature on the platform. Half of career coaches call it essential; half call
it a desperation signal that costs you negotiating leverage. LinkedIn's own resolution is telling —
they shipped a **"recruiters only"** mode, i.e. a share that is *invisible to your social graph*.
The product's answer to its own share feature was to hide it from the people you know.

**So: a share button on "I'm job hunting" is not an untested idea. It is a tested one, and the
verdict is contested at best.** Any ticket that reinvents it needs to say what it does differently.

### What survives the inversion

The rule that gets us out: **the artefact must carry the competence, not the search.**

Strava posts the run, not the anxiety about being unfit. The share has to be a thing that would be
just as postable by someone perfectly happy in their job. If the artefact only makes sense coming
from someone who needs work, it broadcasts need, and need is not social currency.

### The win moments this product has, scored against that rule

| Moment | What the artefact would say | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Profile constellation grows** | "Here is the shape of what I've done" — a self-portrait, no search implied. Our nearest thing to Wrapped, and it is already visual. | **Strongest candidate.** Postable by anyone, employed or not. |
| **A skill/claim verified** | "This is evidenced, not asserted" — competence, conferred by the system rather than claimed by the person. Berger's conferred-status shape exactly. | **Strong**, as a component of the above. |
| **Interview prep completed** | "I can answer this" — competence, but the activity implies an imminent interview, i.e. a search. | **Middling.** Works privately or to a chosen person; risky in a feed. |
| **Tailored CV produced** | Reveals the search *and* the specific target role, and the document itself is private. | **Worst.** Do not build a public share here. |
| **Application streak / volume** | Strava-shaped, but the effort being displayed is *applying for jobs* — pure need signal. | **Bad publicly.** Possibly fine inside a private peer cohort. |
| **Someone gets hired** | "I'm starting a new role" — the single most socially celebrated announcement in professional life. Zero stigma. | **The real prize. See below.** |

### The hire is the share moment, and it needs a Trojan Horse

The hire announcement is a post people already make, voluntarily, with enthusiasm, to exactly the
audience we want, at the exact moment their credibility about how they got there is highest. We do
not have to invent the occasion or overcome any reluctance — the post happens with or without us.

The whole problem reduces to Berger's valuable-virality test: **can they tell that story and leave
us out of it?** Today, yes — trivially. Nothing about a JobCrush-produced application survives into
the moment of celebration. That is the gap worth charting, and it is a *narrative* design problem,
not a share-button problem.

### The channel correction

Berger's 7%-online figure is the most actionable thing in the book for a stigma domain. The share
that matters here is almost certainly **one person telling one person, privately** — a DM to a
friend who is also looking, a mention in a group chat, a reply to "how did you find that job?"

That reframes the build. It is not "add a Story card." It is: *what is the compact, sendable thing
a person hands to one other person, and does it work when pasted into a chat with no preview?*
Wordle's grid is the model — plain text, no link, survives any surface.

### Practical Value is the lever that needs no disclosure at all

Berger's fifth principle is the only one that sidesteps the disclosure problem completely, because
**the sharer does not have to be a job seeker.** A person forwards a useful market fact to the one
friend it applies to, and reveals nothing about themselves.

His narrow-targeting finding is the important half: specific beats general, because a specific fact
summons a specific recipient. "Salaries are up" gets forwarded to nobody. "Delivery managers in
Hong Kong are asked for Jira in 8 of 10 adverts this month" gets forwarded to the one person it is
about. We produce facts of exactly that shape as a by-product of the market work already built.

### Public, at zero cost

The strongest self-advertising surface this product already owns is **the rendered CV itself** —
every one goes to a recruiter, i.e. an audience of precisely the right people, no feed required.
That is the Lululemon-bag position.

Real costs before anyone gets excited: it must not perturb ATS parsing, and a visible mark on a
person's CV may make *them* look worse, which violates the whole principle. Flagging it as a real
option with a real objection, not as a recommendation.

## Open questions for the wayfinder pass

1. **Is the constellation card the artefact?** It is the only thing we have that is identity rather
   than search. Does it read as impressive to a stranger who does not know the product?
2. **What does the compact text form look like?** The Wordle-grid question: what survives being
   pasted into WhatsApp with no link and no image?
3. **What is the trigger?** All three cases have a calendar (daily puzzle / weekly run / December).
   A job search has no natural cadence and *ends* — which is a growth problem no share format
   solves. Is there a recurring moment, or is this one-shot by nature?
4. **What is the honest emotion at each win?** Berger's arousal finding says relief and contentment
   — the likeliest true feelings at "your CV is ready" — are the two that *suppress* sharing. If we
   cannot find awe or excitement, the moment is not a share moment however good the card looks.
5. **How does JobCrush become unretellable-without-us in a hire announcement?**
6. **Does anything here get built before the product has enough users for sharing to matter?**
   Worth asking plainly. A perfect loop over a small base returns nearly nothing, and this is
   design effort that competes with the run order.
